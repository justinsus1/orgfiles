How does Almond use characters to explore the mysteries of life and the
human experience?

When authors create characters that undergo growth and journey through
life, it enables the reader to vicariously explore the mysteries of life
and the human experience. In David Almond's magical realism novella
“Skellig” (1998) follows Almond's 3 main protagonists, Michael, Mina,
and Skellig, exploring the mysteries of life and the human
experience.This is realised through, Almond's portrayal of rebirth and
hope within death, as well as the power of nurture and care through the
characters actions.

Almond explores the duality of life and death, challenging the
traditional precept of death being inherently bad and illustrating the
hope within death. Influenced by his traumatic upbringing filled with
death, Almond implores the reader to vicariously explore the key theme
of death and rebirth. Almond introduces the key theme through the
oxymoron “Doctor Death” collapses the barrier between life and death as
traditionally a doctor is someone who saves lives, connoting life, this
illustrate the duality of life and death and plants the idea within the
reader enabling Almond to build from this foundation throughout the
book. The visual imagery “I thought he was dead” presents Skellig's
abject mental and physical state, illustrating the despair of the
situation. However, the abstract diction of “thought” constructs
ambiguity and depicts a thread of hope within the abject body of Skellig
for transformation and renewal. The metaphor “Then he turned and faced
the daylight”, represents Skellig's drastic transformation as “turned”
connotes an intentional action and “daylight” connotes life, hope, and
renewal, it illustrates Skellig moving away from “darkenss” or death and
moving toward life through renewal. This synergises with the previous
quote, completing the full picture of transformation, illustrating that
death or pushing your boundaries is necessary for rebirth or growth.
Ultimately, Almond challenges traditional precepts of death being
inherently bad, claiming that pushing your comfort zone is necessary for
growth metaphorically.

Almond explores the transformative power of nurture and care,
emphasising the importance and effect of care towards those who need
help. Influenced by his traumatic childhood filled with drama Almond
implores readers to vicariously explore the effect and transformative
power of nurture and care. The characterisation “You can't just sit
there like that you're waiting to die” illustrates Michael's care for
Skellig, the high modality “can't” depicts Michael's desperation in his
pleading, begging Skellig to live, constructing a frustrated tone and
mood. Afterwards, Skellig agrees to live on however, the visual image
“covered in cracked and crooked feathers” represents Skellig's
vulnerability and that change is slow, painful, and not immediately
rewarding evoking a sympathetic tone, mood, and atmosphere. The metaphor
“Then he turned to face the daylight” represents Skellig's drastic
transformation, as “turned” connotes deliberate action, illustrating the
Skellig transformed and re-birthed. This synergises with the previous
quotes to illustrate the gradual effect of nurture, care, and patience
as Skeliig goes from an abject being on the brink of death to vulnerable
to an extraordinary being, representing the key theme of nurture and
care. Ultimately, Almond explores the transformative power of nurture
and care, emphasising the importance and effect of it towards those who
need it.

Therefore, Almond utilises Michael, Mina, and Skellig to position the
reader to learn vicariously with them, experiencing the mysteries of
life and the human experience as they learn to value life and death as
one, and nurture and care.
