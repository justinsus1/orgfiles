*** Importance of Nile

The Nile river was of vital importance to Egyptian Society. It spanned
over 6650 km and it coursed through the countries of Africa, connecting
the Mediterranean sea with the Indian Ocean. The Nile's flow of water
provided Egyptians with fertile soil usable for agriculture, allowing
farmers to grow a stable supply of food, and a constant, safe supply of
drinking water. The course of the river also enabled Egyptians to
transport goods over vast distances, throughout their country, building
a strong economy. Finally, the river was a source of spirituality,
allowing the Egyptian people to build faith, sharing a common belief. 

To begin with, the Nile would deposit rich silt that was suitable for
agriculture annually onto the land with its flood. The silt from the bed
of the Nile river was rich in nutrients and minerals. When flooded
through the farmlands, farmers could grow healthy and nutritious crops,
such as wheat and barley. These reliable grains formed into a staple of
the Egyptian diet. With the annual floods, Egyptian mathematicians built
nilometers, enabling them to predict the timing and extent of floods.
This allowed them to prepare, and recover much more efficiently. This
led some advanced Egyptians to build basin irrigation systems to store
flood water to later redistribute among their crops. Finally, the Nile,
being a river, permitted the Egyptians to fish in it, supplying the past
Egyptian civilization with a stable food source. Together, these
properties of the Nile River made Egypt and its people rich in
agriculture, and was one of their main sources of wealth.

The Nile river was also a key transportation and economic hub for the
population. With its extensive course and strong flow, the river enabled
the ancient Egyptians to transport goods and even people over vast
distances. With advanced navigators and captains, Egyptian businessmen
could be easily transported through the river, spanning over 6650 km,
without any source of fuel. Instead, they relied on basic sailing
principles, such as water current and wind. The ancient Egyptians would
transfer grains and fish to trade with other nations such as ancient
Nubia, and Mesopotamia for materials such as gold and ivory. Together,
the Nile was utilised to transport goods/people for trade and
communication.

Additionally,  the Nile river played an influential role in the ancient
Egyptian's spiritual life. Hapi, also known as the “running one” was
considered the god of the Nile river (Jacaranda History, 2018). The
annual flood of the Nile was often referred to as the “arrival of Hapi”.
Hapi was characterised by large female breasts, a large stomach, and
blue skin symbolising the fertility of the Nile, and in turn, the gift
of fertility to the people using the river. For these reasons, many
Egyptians built temples and gave Hapi offerings, praying the annual
flood of the Nile would be at the right water levels for their crops.
Therefore, the Nile played an important role in the spiritual lives of
the ancient Egyptians.

Ancient Egypt was a major powerhouse country in the ancient world,
influencing not only the ancient world but also modern society. The Nile
played a crucial role in this success and the Egyptians location enabled
them to rule over the Nile, it allowed them to enjoy immense wealth from
agricultural trading, fast communication, and facilitated simple
transportation of goods throughout North Africa. Ultimately, the Nile
was vital to the Egyptians and enabled them to thrive throughout the
better part of history effortlessly and conquer Northern African.

*** Beliefs of the Egyptians

:END:
Throughout Egyptian History, religion has been an important factor in
society of all levels. This was seen in the people practicing rituals
such as the mummification of the dead, and elaborate burial practices
for royals as they prepare for the next life in the afterlife. These
beliefs played a major role in both their political and everyday life.

To begin with, Egyptians believed that death was not the end of a human
being, but rather a pit stop as they advance to the afterlife. The
afterlife can be split into 2 outcomes, an affable heavenly paradise
known as Aaru (the Field of reeds), or devoured by Ammit (the eater of
the dead) (Mark, 2019). However, these outcomes could only be done
through preservation of the body to act as a sanctuary for the soul,
enabling it to rest and receive food offerings; otherwise it was
believed that the soul would become lost and unable to advance to Aaru
(Mark, 2019). To practice this belief, the highest members of society,
the pharaohs, would receive a mummification process lasting up to 70
days. The process begins with washing the body using water from the Nile
River, and rubbing oil and other spices, purifying it. Afterwards, the
brain, stomach, liver, lungs, and intestines were removed for separate,
individual preservation. The heart was not removed as it was considered
the seat of intelligence and essential for the migration of the soul.
Finally the body would be dehydrated with Natron (naturally occurring
salt mixture), for a period of 40-70 days, before being washed again and
wrapped, while maintaining a lifelike appearance. This was an elaborate
and expensive process, that only was afforded to the highest of
royalties. For the more affluent Egyptians, a shorter version of the
process was practiced. And for the general population, a majority was
buried in sand, which naturally dried and preserved the body (Anderson,
Keese and Low, 2018).

Furthermore, the Egyptians had unique burial practices related to their
beliefs of the afterlife. One of the most prominent and unique practices
included the building of massive, elaborate tombs for their pharaohs
known as pyramids. Egyptians believed that these elaborate tombs would
serve as an extra push to facilitate their migration to Aaru. Moreover,
the pyramids would contain food, and treasure, as it was believed that
you could take offerings into the other life to live comfortably, as a
result many tombs of pharaohs were often ornately decorated (Australian
Museum, 2018). The Egyptians built these pyramids 0ver a predicted span
of around 20 years; the Egyptians would employ simple machines to
realise this dream, such as ramps, pulleys, and levers, this drastically
facilitated the construction of the pyramids (Handwerk, 2023). These
pyramids were so intricate and well built that to this day, they remain
standing structures in Egypt. They are now considered one of the great
wonders of the world (National Geographic Society, 2022), where millions
of people still flock to each year to appreciate the technology and
technique of Egyptians past. 

Many past societies, including that of Egyptians, had elaborate and
monumental religious practices. These are still practiced by some
societies in today's modern day. Ancient Egypt and their pyramids still
play a massive role in everyday Egyptians lives, and the pyramids still
stand proud, as evidence for their great achievements. Therefore, the
ancient Egyptian religion played a massive role in everyday life and the
Egyptians, and still maintains a tight hold on historians today.

redistribution at will (Anderson, Keese, and Low, 2018). Through these
